Not long ago, I remember spending hours scrolling through documentation and reading through forum threads to find an answer that fit *my* specific problem. Today, the same investigation takes minutes using an AI that has read more code than I ever will. It is fast, fluent, and occasionally brilliant. But every time I use it, I can't shake the feeling that something important is shifting underneath the profession I've spent years learning.

This post is my attempt to think that through out loud: how AI affects us as programmers, whether learning to program still makes sense, and — if it does — what the best way to learn looks like now.

## How AI has changed programming

The most obvious change is in the daily loop. It all started with Auto-completion as a feature in IDEs and has grown to full-fledged AI Assistants. The boilerplate code that used to take an entire morning is now being generated in a single minute. Most of the grinding that was required for me to come up with a working function, that catered for my specific task, has been completely replaced with how well I can prompt the AI agent. AI has absorbed the most routine parts of the job, writing the code.

The aspect of writing code has been greatly reduced making the review part the most important one. The work has shifted from writing to judging. I now spend far more of my day reading AI-generated code and deciding whether it's correct, secure and a good fit. This has freed the time used for writing the code and has allowed us to focus on the overall system design and architecture, making us work at a higher level.

The pace of expectation has changed. When assigned a feature, the stakeholders reasonably ask why it took two weeks to implement it. Speed is now taken for granted and the value we bring to the table has moved to correctness, trade-offs and the non-obvious problems that AI still struggles with.  

The changes that AI has led to in the programming field are enormous and the profession is being reshaped. The routine task of implementation is being commoditized and what is left is the part of the job that was always the real job — understanding systems, making decisions,  and owning outcomes.

## Should you still learn programming?

Over the past few years, we've all come across the question: *"If AI can write code, why should I bother learning?"* Why should anyone go through the troubles of learning how to program if AI is going to be doing most of the work?

Short answer: you should. 

You can’t direct what you don’t understand. When you work with an AI, your output is only as good as your instructions, and your instructions are only as good as your mental model. If you can’t predict roughly what the machine will produce, you can’t evaluate it, debug it, or know when to overrule it. The AI doesn’t remove the need to understand systems — it makes that understanding the entire job. AI is pushing programmers up, away from the mechanics of syntax and toward design, architecture, security and domain expertise.

There’s also something quieter at stake. Learning to program teaches you how to think — how to decompose a messy problem into pieces, express yourself with precision, and prove to yourself that something works. Those habits don’t go obsolete. They’re the exact skills that separate someone who uses AI from someone who leads it.

## The best way to learn now

So what does serious learning look like in the age of AI? The same as it always did:

**Learn the fundamentals before you outsource them.** Study data structures and algorithms, learn how a computer actually executes a program, and write code with your own hands before you lean on autocomplete. These aren't nostalgic requirements; they're your defense against becoming a person who can only prompt. Early on, I'd go further and write whole programs without AI help — not as a purist statement, but because struggling through a bug *is* how the mental model gets built. Skipping the struggle skips the learning.

Beyond that, I'd sketch the practical path like this:

1. **Build real things, end to end.** Deploy something — a CLI tool, a small web app, a scraper — and maintain it. Real constraints are where learning actually happens.
2. **Read code and run it.** Reading other people's code is how you absorb taste — and running it, changing it, breaking it is how you absorb understanding.
3. **Learn to be skeptical of AI output.** Verify, test, review, and question. Treat the assistant's answer as a *draft from a confident colleague*, not as truth. The skill of catching hallucinations is now a core professional skill.
4. **Use AI as a tutor, not a substitute.** Ask it *why*, ask for the trade-offs, ask it to explain the concept behind the code. The best questions are the ones that end with a conversation, not a copy-paste.
5. **Keep a no-AI practice.** Deliberately solve problems of increasing difficulty without assistance. It keeps your fundamentals sharp and reminds you what you actually know.

A useful mental rule is this: **ask AI for explanations almost always and for answers only when you already know what a good answer looks like.**

## A small closing thought

I like to think the profession is entering its most interesting phase. The scut work is dissolving, which means the people who remain are being judged on understanding, judgement, and the quality of their decisions. That's intimidating. It's also the most honest version of this craft I've ever seen.

So yes, learn to program. Learn it deeply. Use every tool you can get your hands on — but treat AI as the pair programmer you argue with, not the author you defer to. The code belongs to you either way, and so does the responsibility for it.

The tools change. The craft doesn't.
